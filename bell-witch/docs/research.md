# Research notes: the Bell Witch

The legend of the Bell family farm on the Red River, Robertson County (now Adams), Tennessee,
1817-1821. Primary written account: M. V. Ingram, *An Authenticated History of the Famous Bell Witch*
(1894); earlier mention in Goodspeed's *History of Tennessee* (1886). Folklorists (Nickell, Dunning)
question Ingram's reliability; the film tells it as legend.

What the film uses, and where it comes from:
- 1817: John Bell sees a strange creature "resembling a dog" in his cornfield, fires at it, and it
  vanishes. (Wikipedia, Tennessee Encyclopedia; the rabbit-headed description is later folklore, so the
  film shows only a dog-shape with long ears in fog.)
- Knocking on the walls, gnawing on the bedposts, chains dragged across the floor, sheets pulled from
  the beds, the children slapped and pinched. Goodspeed (1886): "It would take the sugar from the bowls,
  spill the milk, take the quilts from the beds, slap and pinch the children."
- The voice, asked who it was: "I am a spirit; I was once very happy but have been disturbed."
  (Ingram, ch. 8, quoted on Wikipedia.) It answered to "Kate" after calling itself Old Kate Batts' witch.
- It hated John Bell ("Old Jack"). December 1820: a vial of dark liquid is found in the cupboard; a drop
  given to the cat kills it; thrown in the fire, the vial "flashed a blue flame". John Bell dies
  December 20, 1820.
- At his burial the spirit sang "Row me up some brandy, O" (Mahala Darden's account, Ingram ch. 8).
- 1821: it leaves, promising to return in seven years; it returns in 1828 to Lucy Bell and her sons.
  It then promises a return 107 years later (1935, per Charles Bailey Bell, 1934). No return was recorded.
